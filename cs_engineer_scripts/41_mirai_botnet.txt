In 2016, a large part of the US internet went dark, Twitter, Netflix, Reddit, Spotify, all unreachable, taken down by an army of hacked security cameras and home routers. Not sophisticated supercomputers. Cheap internet-connected gadgets, hundreds of thousands of them, hijacked using nothing more than the factory-default passwords their owners never changed. The weapon was a program called Mirai, and it's the clearest warning ever delivered about the security of the internet of things, and about how small, dumb devices become a giant hammer.

Start with the attack type, because that's the goal everything serves. A distributed denial-of-service attack, a DDoS, works by directing an overwhelming flood of traffic at a target from many machines at once, so much that the target is buried and legitimate users can't get through. It's not breaking in, it's drowning. The power of a DDoS comes from how many machines you can point at the target and how much traffic they can collectively generate. So an attacker wants an army, a botnet, a large collection of machines they secretly control, all firing at once on command.

Mirai's insight was where to find a cheap, enormous army: the internet of things. By 2016, the world was filling up with internet-connected devices, security cameras, home routers, DVRs, baby monitors, built cheaply, shipped fast, and secured terribly. The specific weakness Mirai exploited was almost embarrassingly simple. These devices came with default usernames and passwords set at the factory, things like admin and password, and buyers almost never changed them, many didn't even know they could. Mirai did nothing clever. It just scanned the internet for these devices and tried to log in using a built-in list of about 60 common factory default username-and-password combinations. For a vast number of devices, one of those defaults simply worked. No exploit, no breaking of encryption, just walking in through a door that was never locked.

Once in, Mirai installed itself on the device, which then quietly joined the botnet, waiting for orders while the owner noticed nothing, their camera still worked fine. And it kept scanning for more devices to infect, growing itself the way a worm does. In this way Mirai assembled an army of hundreds of thousands of compromised gadgets. Individually, a single hacked camera can send only a trickle of traffic. But the internet of things is vast, so hundreds of thousands of trickles, fired simultaneously at one target, became a torrent. When Mirai aimed this army, it produced some of the largest DDoS attacks ever seen at the time, hitting a well-known security journalist's site, and then striking a company called Dyn that provided DNS, the internet's address-lookup service, for many major sites. Knock out the DNS provider, and all the sites relying on it become unreachable even though they're perfectly fine, which is why one attack darkened so much of the internet at once. And then Mirai's author released the source code publicly, so copycats spread it everywhere.

And if you build connected things, this part is on you. Never ship a device with a default password that works out of the box, force the user to set a unique one on first use, because you cannot rely on people to change defaults themselves. Assume every device you put on the internet will be scanned and probed within minutes, because Mirai and its descendants are out there right now, constantly trying default logins against everything with an IP address. And recognize the broader principle: in security, you don't get to pick the battlefield, attackers target the weakest entry point, and a fleet of cheap gadgets with the same factory password is about as weak as it gets. The strength of your system is the strength of its weakest reachable device, multiplied by how many of them there are.

Security cameras took down Twitter and Netflix, not with genius, but with factory passwords nobody changed, hundreds of thousands of them firing at once. Small and dumb becomes huge and dangerous in bulk. Lock the door you shipped, because something is already trying the handle.
