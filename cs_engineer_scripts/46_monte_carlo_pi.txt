You can calculate pi by throwing darts at a board while blindfolded. No circles measured, no formula for pi used anywhere. Just random darts and counting. It sounds like a trick, but it's a serious technique called Monte Carlo, and it runs real simulations in finance, physics, and engineering every day. The catch is in how slowly it gets accurate, and that catch is the real lesson.

Here's the setup. Picture a square, one unit on each side, and inside it a quarter of a circle, centered at one corner, with radius one, sweeping from one side to the other. Now throw darts at the square completely at random, each dart landing at a random spot, uniformly, anywhere in the square. For each dart, you can check whether it landed inside the quarter circle with nothing more than the Pythagorean theorem: a dart at position x, y is inside if x squared plus y squared is less than or equal to one. No pi needed for that test, just multiplication and addition.

Now count. What fraction of your darts land inside the quarter circle? It should match the fraction of the square's area that the quarter circle covers. The square has area one. The quarter circle has area pi times radius squared, divided by four, which with radius one is just pi over four. So the fraction of darts inside should be about pi over four. Throw a thousand darts, see what fraction landed inside, multiply that fraction by four, and you have an estimate of pi. The darts never know what pi is. They just fill the space evenly, and the circle's area reveals pi through the count.

Throw more darts and the estimate gets better, because random points, in bulk, fill the square evenly and the ratio settles toward the true area. But here's the part every engineer needs to know, the part that decides whether Monte Carlo is the right tool: how fast does it improve? The error shrinks in proportion to one over the square root of the number of darts. Sit with what that means. To cut your error in half, you need four times as many darts. To get one more correct decimal digit of pi, a tenfold improvement in accuracy, you need a hundred times more darts. To get yet another digit, ten thousand times more. The accuracy crawls while the work explodes. This is a slow, expensive way to compute pi, and nobody computes pi this way in practice.

So why does Monte Carlo matter at all, if it's this slow? Because of one property that square-root-of-N error has: it does not care how many dimensions the problem has. Our dart problem is two-dimensional, x and y. But suppose you're pricing a financial option that depends on fifty uncertain variables, or simulating neutrons bouncing through a reactor in three dimensions over time. The traditional approach, laying down a regular grid and sampling every cell, explodes with dimensions: a grid of just ten steps per variable, across fifty variables, is ten to the fiftieth cells, impossible. That's the curse of dimensionality. Monte Carlo's error stays at one over the square root of the sample count whether the problem has two dimensions or two hundred. Random sampling sidesteps the grid explosion entirely.

So the real lesson is about choosing your tool by the shape of the problem. For one or two dimensions where you want many digits, Monte Carlo is the worst choice, far too slow. For a high-dimensional problem where a grid is hopeless and you only need a few digits of accuracy, it's often the only choice that works at all. The slow square-root convergence is the price, and in high dimensions it's a price nothing else can beat.

Random darts, a count, and a multiply by four. A terrible way to find pi, and a brilliant way to simulate a world too tangled to grid. Same method, and knowing which situation you're in is the whole skill.
