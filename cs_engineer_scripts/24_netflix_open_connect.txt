At peak hours, Netflix can account for a huge share of all the internet traffic on entire continents. Streaming that much video to that many people at once should be impossible, it should saturate the internet's backbone and buffer for everyone. Netflix's solution was to not send most of that video across the internet at all. Instead, they put the movies inside your internet provider's building, before you ever pressed play. The system is called Open Connect, and it's the ultimate expression of a principle we keep meeting: move the data close to the user, ahead of demand.

Start with why the obvious approach fails. The naive way to run a streaming service is to keep all your video in a few big data centers and stream it out to everyone from there. But think about the traffic. Millions of people watching high-definition video simultaneously is an enormous, continuous river of data. If all of it flowed from a central location, across the long-distance links of the internet, through many networks, to reach each viewer, you'd be pushing petabytes across the internet's shared backbone every evening. Those long-haul links would congest, costs would be astronomical, and quality would suffer, because the further data travels and the more congested links it crosses, the more it stutters. You simply cannot stream the world's video from a central source over the public internet at this scale.

The key realization is that video is special in two ways that unlock a solution. First, the content is the same for everyone, millions of people watch the identical popular show, so it makes no sense to fetch it fresh from far away for each viewer. Second, you know in advance, roughly, what will be popular, the new hit series, the trending films, because viewing patterns are predictable. Put those together and a strategy appears: instead of streaming on demand from afar, pre-position copies of the popular content physically close to viewers, before they ask for it.

That's exactly what Open Connect does. Netflix built specialized caching servers, called Open Connect Appliances, boxes packed with storage, and placed thousands of them directly inside the networks of internet providers around the world, and at major internet exchange points. So there's a box full of Netflix content sitting right in your ISP's facility, a short hop from your home. Then, crucially, during the quiet overnight hours when the network is mostly idle, Netflix proactively fills these boxes with the content it predicts will be watched the next day, pushing the new episodes and trending films out to every appliance in advance. When you press play the next evening, the video doesn't travel across the internet from a distant data center, it comes from that box inside your provider's network, meters away in internet terms. The massive evening streaming load is served locally, from thousands of caches near the edge, instead of from the center.

Look at what this achieves. The heavy traffic, serving the actual video, never touches the congested long-distance backbone, because the content is already local when you want it. It travels only the short final stretch from your provider to you. The internet's core is spared, your provider's external links are spared, and you get a smooth, fast stream from nearby. And the bandwidth used to fill the boxes was spent overnight, during idle time, so it didn't compete with anyone. Netflix turned a seemingly impossible real-time bandwidth problem into a storage-and-prediction problem: keep copies everywhere, and fill them when the network is free.

This is the content delivery network idea, which we've seen for web pages and live sport, pushed to its logical extreme and specialized for video. The twist that makes Open Connect distinct is the proactive placement, not caching content after the first person requests it, but predicting demand and pre-loading it during off-peak hours, so the cache is already warm before the rush. That's trading cheap storage and smart prediction against expensive, scarce peak bandwidth.

Netflix is a huge fraction of the internet's evening traffic, and most of it never crosses the internet. The movie was sitting in your provider's building before you pressed play, loaded overnight while the network slept. Move the data close, ahead of demand, and the impossible rush becomes a local handoff.
