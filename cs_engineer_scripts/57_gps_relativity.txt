The GPS in your phone would be useless, drifting its location by about 10 kilometers a day, if it didn't correct for Einstein's theory of relativity. This is the most concrete, everyday proof that relativity is real engineering, not abstract physics. The satellites overhead literally keep time differently than you do, and GPS only works because engineers built Einstein's equations into the system. Here's the mechanism, number by number.

Start with how GPS finds you, because the whole thing rests on time. Each GPS satellite carries an extremely precise atomic clock and constantly broadcasts the message "here is exactly what time it is, and here is where I am." Your phone receives signals from several satellites. Since the signals travel at the speed of light, your phone figures out how far away each satellite is by measuring how long the signal took to arrive, distance equals the speed of light times the travel time. With distances to several satellites, it triangulates your position. The entire system is really a way of measuring tiny time delays, and converting them to distance. And because light travels about 30 centimeters in a single nanosecond, the timing has to be accurate to nanoseconds, or the position is wrong. GPS is, at its heart, a clock measurement.

Now the relativity, and there are two separate effects pulling in opposite directions. The first comes from special relativity, which says a clock that is moving ticks slower than one standing still. GPS satellites orbit at about 14,000 kilometers per hour, so from our point of view on the ground, their fast-moving clocks run slow, losing about 7 microseconds per day relative to clocks on Earth. Seven millionths of a second a day, from their speed.

The second effect comes from general relativity, which says that gravity slows time, so a clock deeper in a gravitational well, closer to a massive body, ticks slower, and a clock higher up, where gravity is weaker, ticks faster. The satellites orbit about 20,000 kilometers up, where Earth's gravity is much weaker than at the surface. So their clocks, being higher in the gravitational well, run fast, gaining about 45 microseconds per day relative to the ground. Forty-five millionths of a second a day, from being up high.

Put the two together. Special relativity makes the satellite clocks lose 7 microseconds a day, general relativity makes them gain 45. The net effect is a gain of about 38 microseconds per day, the satellite clocks run 38 millionths of a second per day faster than clocks on the ground. That sounds utterly trivial, and this is the moment that turns a physics curiosity into an engineering necessity.

Do the arithmetic on what 38 microseconds of timing error does to position. Remember light, and the GPS signal, travels about 30 centimeters per nanosecond, which is 300 meters per microsecond. So an uncorrected timing error of 38 microseconds a day translates to a distance error of 38 times 300 meters, which is about 11 kilometers. Per day. If GPS ignored relativity, your position would drift by roughly 10 kilometers every day, and within hours it would be useless for navigation, placing you in the wrong neighborhood, then the wrong city. The tiny clock discrepancy, multiplied by the speed of light, becomes a catastrophic location error almost immediately.

So the engineers who built GPS accounted for it directly. The satellite clocks are deliberately adjusted so that, once in orbit and running at their relativistic rate, they tick at the correct rate as seen from the ground. The relativistic corrections are built into the system from the start. Every time your phone shows your location, Einstein's equations are quietly doing their job overhead.

Your phone knows where you are because its engineers trusted Einstein. The satellites' clocks really do run faster than yours, by 38 millionths of a second a day, and left uncorrected that would put you 10 kilometers from where you stand. Relativity isn't abstract. It's in your pocket, keeping you on the right street.
