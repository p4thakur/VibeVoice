In October 2018, GitHub broke for over 24 hours because of a network problem that lasted 43 seconds. Forty-three seconds of confusion, more than a day of cleanup. The reason that tiny blip caused such lasting damage is a failure mode called split brain, and it's one of the scariest things that can happen to a database, because it's the moment a system forgets there's supposed to be only one source of truth.

Start with the normal setup. For safety, important databases are replicated: one node is the leader, which accepts all the writes, and others are followers, which copy the leader's data so they can take over if it dies. The critical rule is that there must be exactly one leader at a time. The leader is the single source of truth. Followers defer to it. This is what keeps everyone's view of the data consistent.

Now the dangerous situation. What happens if the network splits, if a link between data centers goes down and the nodes can suddenly no longer all talk to each other? Say the leader is on one side of the split, and the followers on the other side can no longer reach it. From the followers' point of view, the leader has vanished, it's not responding, so it must be dead. And they're built to handle a dead leader: they elect a new one among themselves. Reasonable. But here's the catastrophe: the old leader isn't actually dead. It's alive and well on its side of the split, still accepting writes, because from its point of view, it's fine, it's the followers that disappeared. Now you have two leaders. Both think they're the one true source of truth. Both accept writes. This is split brain.

And it's poison, because the two leaders now diverge. A user on one side updates their profile on leader A. A job on the other side writes conflicting data to leader B. Each leader records changes the other never sees. The single source of truth has become two sources that disagree, and there is no automatic, correct way to reconcile them once the network heals, because both sets of writes are "real." Someone's data has to lose, or be painstakingly merged by hand. That's exactly what happened to GitHub: a brief partition let their database clusters diverge, and the 24 hours was the agonizing work of reconciling the conflicting writes safely, not the outage itself. The blip caused the split; the divergence caused the pain.

So how do real systems prevent two leaders? The core idea is quorum, requiring a majority. You don't let a group elect a new leader unless it can see more than half of all the nodes. Picture five nodes split into a group of three and a group of two. The group of three has a majority, so it may elect a leader and keep serving. The group of two does not have a majority, so it refuses to elect a leader and stops accepting writes, it makes itself unavailable rather than risk becoming a second brain. Because a cluster can only ever have one majority, you can never get two leaders. The price is visible and deliberate: the minority side goes offline. The system chooses to stop serving on one side rather than to serve inconsistently, a direct expression of the CAP tradeoff, under a partition, you pick consistency or availability, and here you pick consistency.

There's a second safeguard called fencing: when a new leader is elected, the old one must be reliably stopped or blocked from writing, sometimes bluntly, so that even if it wakes up thinking it's still in charge, its writes are rejected. A common mechanism is a fencing token, a number that increases with each new leadership, so storage can refuse writes stamped with an old, lower token.

Forty-three seconds of split network. Twenty-four hours of untangling two databases that both thought they were in charge. One leader, enforced by a majority, is not a detail. It's the whole game.
