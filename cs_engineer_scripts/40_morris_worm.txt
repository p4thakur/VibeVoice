On November 2nd, 1988, a graduate student released a small program onto the early internet as an experiment. Within hours it had crashed around 6,000 machines, roughly ten percent of the entire internet at the time, and parts of the network had to be cut off from each other to stop it. The program carried no destructive payload, it wasn't trying to delete anything, and yet it caused the first great internet disaster. The reason it did so much damage comes down to one number the author chose wrong, and that number is a beautiful, precise lesson about replication.

Start with what the Morris worm was and wanted to do. Robert Morris, a student, wrote a self-replicating program, a worm, that would spread from computer to computer across the internet. Its stated purpose was almost innocent: to gauge the size of the internet by quietly copying itself onto as many machines as it could. It spread by exploiting known weaknesses in Unix systems of the day, including a buffer overflow in a network program, the same class of bug as the Pac-Man kill screen, writing past the end of a buffer to hijack a machine, and weak remote-access paths. A machine would get infected, and from there the worm would reach out and try to infect others. So far, in intent, this was reconnaissance, not sabotage.

Now the fatal design decision, and this is the whole story. Morris anticipated a defense: system administrators might write a fake version of the worm to fool the real one into thinking a machine was already infected, so it would skip that machine and stop spreading. To counter that, he made the worm sometimes reinfect a machine even if it appeared to already be infected. Specifically, when the worm found a machine that claimed to already have it, it would reinfect anyway one time out of seven. That one-in-seven was meant to be cautious, a small chance, just enough to defeat fake immunizations without reinfecting too aggressively. It turned out to be catastrophically too high.

Here's why. Think about what happens on a popular, well-connected machine on the early internet. It isn't contacted by the worm once, it's contacted over and over, by every infected machine that reaches it. Each time, there's a one-in-seven chance of a fresh reinfection, a whole new copy of the worm starting up as its own running process. So these machines didn't get infected once, they got infected dozens, then hundreds of times, each copy consuming memory and processor time. The machines weren't damaged by any malicious instruction, there wasn't one, they were ground to a halt by running an ever-growing pile of copies of the worm, until they became unusable and crashed. The damage was pure replication, multiplied by that one-in-seven constant, run across a busy network. A slightly different number, one in a hundred, say, and the worm might have spread quietly as intended and barely been noticed. One in seven turned it into a self-inflicted denial-of-service that took down a tenth of the internet.

The response was drastic: administrators disconnected their networks from each other to stop the spread and clean up, which fragmented the early internet for days. Morris became the first person convicted under America's new computer fraud law. And the incident directly led to the creation of the first coordinated computer-security response team, the organization that still coordinates responses to major vulnerabilities today.

And the sharpest part: the whole catastrophe hinged on a single probability constant, one in seven, chosen with a plausible-sounding rationale that was quantitatively wrong for the real environment. Morris reasoned correctly that he needed some reinfection to beat fakes, but badly misjudged how often busy machines would be contacted, so his safety parameter became the detonator. It's a permanent reminder that in systems involving multiplication, feedback, or spread, the exact value of your constants matters enormously, and a number that feels conservative in your head can be explosive in production. Always ask: if this gets triggered far more often than I expect, what happens?

A program with no intent to harm took down a tenth of the internet, because it copied itself one time in seven when it should have almost never. No payload, just replication without a brake. Put a governor on anything that multiplies, and check your constants against the real world, not the one in your head.
