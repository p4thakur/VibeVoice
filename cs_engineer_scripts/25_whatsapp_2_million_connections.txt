WhatsApp once served around 450 million users, carrying tens of billions of messages a day, with an engineering team of about 32 people. And a single one of their servers held 2 million live connections at once, millions of phones all maintaining an open line to one machine. Those numbers sound impossible until you understand the two choices behind them: the right concurrency model, and the discipline to keep the server doing almost nothing per user. It's a lesson in how the right foundation lets a tiny team outscale armies.

Start with the technical mountain: a messaging app needs every phone to keep a live, open connection to the server, so messages can arrive the instant they're sent, without the phone constantly asking "anything for me?" So the server must hold millions of connections open simultaneously, mostly idle, each just waiting for the occasional message. This is the C10k problem we met, scaled up massively, to millions per machine. The usual approach, one operating-system thread per connection, dies here instantly, millions of threads would need terabytes of memory and would drown the processor in switching between them, as we saw.

WhatsApp's first great choice was the language and runtime: Erlang. Erlang was built decades ago by a phone company for exactly this kind of problem, systems that must handle enormous numbers of simultaneous, independent, long-lived conversations reliably. Its magic is a concurrency model built on extremely lightweight processes, not operating-system threads. An Erlang process is tiny, using a few hundred bytes, and the Erlang runtime, not the operating system, schedules millions of them across the processor cores. So WhatsApp could give every single connection its own little Erlang process, millions of them on one machine, each handling one user's connection, isolated from the others so one crashing doesn't touch the rest. The model fits the problem exactly: millions of independent, mostly-waiting conversations map perfectly onto millions of cheap, independent, mostly-idle processes. This is the actor model, and it's why reaching 2 million connections on a single server was even conceivable, they also had to tune the FreeBSD operating system underneath to allow that many open connections, but the concurrency model is what made it natural.

The second great choice was keeping the server's job per message almost trivially small, which kept state low and let the hardware breathe. WhatsApp's core was essentially a message router with store-and-forward: a message comes in, the server figures out the recipient, and if they're online it forwards it immediately, if not it holds it briefly and delivers when they reconnect, then deletes it. The server wasn't storing your whole chat history forever, running heavy features, or doing complex processing on the hot path, it received, routed, delivered, forgot. Because each message cost so little work and the server kept so little per-user state, one machine could handle a staggering number of them. They resisted the temptation to make the server do more, and that restraint is what kept the per-message cost low enough to serve billions a day on modest hardware.

Put the two together and the famous efficiency follows. The right concurrency model, Erlang's lightweight processes, meant millions of connections fit on a machine without the thread overhead that kills naive designs. And the minimal, stateless-as-possible server job meant each of those connections and messages cost almost nothing to handle. Fewer moving parts, less code, less to operate, which is exactly why 32 engineers could run it: the system was simple, and simple systems need fewer people to keep alive. They chose boring, proven technology, a lean design, and powerful primitives, over a sprawling custom architecture.

Four hundred and fifty million users, tens of billions of messages a day, 32 engineers, 2 million connections on one box. Not a miracle of brute force, but the payoff of picking a concurrency model shaped like the problem, and refusing to make the server do more than it had to.
