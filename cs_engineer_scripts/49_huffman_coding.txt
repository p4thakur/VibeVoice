Every time you open a zip file, a JPEG, or an MP3, a piece of a teenager's homework is running inside it. In 1951, a student named David Huffman, offered the choice of taking a final exam or solving an open problem, solved the problem, and his answer is still compressing a huge fraction of the world's data today. It's called Huffman coding, and the idea behind it is one you can reuse far beyond compression: spend your cheapest resource on your most common case.

Start with the waste it fixes. Normally every character in text takes the same space, 8 bits per character, whether it's a common 'e' or a rare 'z'. But letters aren't used equally. In English, 'e' appears constantly, while 'z' and 'q' are rare. Spending the same 8 bits on a letter you use a thousand times and a letter you use once is like charging the same postage for a postcard and a piano. Huffman's question was: what if common characters got short codes, and rare ones got long codes?

Here's how it builds those codes, and the mechanism is elegant. First, count how often each character appears. Then take the two least frequent characters and join them under a little shared branch, a tiny tree with two leaves. Treat that pair as a single combined item whose frequency is the sum of the two. Now repeat: again find the two least-frequent items, where an item might be a single character or a branch you already built, and join them. Keep going until everything is merged into one big tree. The rare characters, joined early, end up deep in the tree, far from the top. The common characters, joined last, sit near the top.

Now read codes off the tree. Starting from the top, every time you go left you write a 0, every time you go right you write a 1, until you reach a character at a leaf. Because common characters are near the top, their paths are short, maybe 2 or 3 bits. Rare characters are deep, so their codes are long, maybe 10 bits. You've replaced fixed 8-bits-each with a scheme where 'e' might cost 3 bits and 'z' costs 10. Since you use 'e' constantly and 'z' almost never, the total shrinks.

There's one property that makes this actually decodable, and it's subtle. No character's code is a prefix of another character's code. The code for 'e' is never the start of the code for some other letter. This is guaranteed automatically, because characters only ever sit at the leaves of the tree, never along the path to another character. Why does that matter? Because it means you can read a stream of bits with no commas or markers between codes, and never be confused about where one character ends and the next begins. You walk down the tree bit by bit; the moment you hit a leaf, that's one character, and you jump back to the top for the next. The prefix-free property is what lets the compressed bits run together with zero wasted space on delimiters.

Huffman coding is provably optimal, for the specific game of assigning a whole number of bits to each individual symbol by its frequency. You cannot do better at that exact task. That's why it's everywhere: it's the final squeeze inside the DEFLATE algorithm that powers zip and gzip and PNG, and it's a stage inside JPEG and MP3. In DEFLATE it's paired with another method that first finds repeated chunks, because Huffman has one limitation: it only knows about single symbols and their frequencies, so it can't exploit the fact that the word "the" repeats, only that 't', 'h', and 'e' are common. Pairing it with repeat-detection covers both.

And Huffman's core move outlives compression: when your costs aren't equal, make the frequent thing cheap and let the rare thing be expensive. It's the same instinct behind putting your hottest data in the fastest cache, and behind optimizing the common code path while tolerating a slow error path. Measure what happens often, and spend your budget there.

A student's exam answer from 1951, still shrinking your files today. Common things get short codes, rare things get long ones, and the whole tree falls out of counting.
