In almost every programming language, there is a value that is not equal to itself. Ask whether it equals itself, and the computer says no. It breaks sorting, it poisons your averages, and it can make a search loop forever. It's called NaN, Not a Number, and it's not a bug in your language. It's a deliberate rule in how computers do decimal math, and knowing it separates engineers who get mysterious data corruption from the ones who catch it.

First, where NaN comes from. Computers store decimal numbers in a format called IEEE 754 floating point. Most of the time it just holds numbers like 3.14. But some operations have no sensible numeric answer. What's zero divided by zero? What's the square root of negative one, in real numbers? What's infinity minus infinity? These aren't just large or small, they're undefined. Floating point needs to return something rather than crash, so it returns a special value that means "the result is not a real number." That's NaN.

Now the strange part. The people who designed the standard decided that NaN is not equal to anything, including another NaN, including itself. So in code, NaN equals NaN comes back false. And NaN is not equal to NaN comes back true. A value genuinely fails to equal itself.

Why would anyone design that? Because NaN doesn't represent one specific wrong number, it represents "unknown, undefined, the computation fell apart." And two undefined things shouldn't be considered the same. If you divide zero by zero over here, and take the square root of a negative over there, both give NaN, but they came from completely different failures. Declaring them equal would be a lie. So the standard says: a NaN matches nothing, not even its own kind. There's even a clever use for it. Before languages had a built-in "is this NaN" function, the way to test was exactly this: ask whether a value is not equal to itself. If yes, it's NaN, because nothing else fails that test.

But here's why this ruins things in practice, and these are real production failures. Suppose one bad sensor reading, or one divide-by-zero, slips a NaN into a list of numbers. Now you sort the list. Sorting works by comparing pairs, is A less than B, and every comparison involving NaN comes back false, A is not less than B and B is not less than A. The sort algorithm gets contradictory answers, its assumptions break, and depending on the language you get a scrambled list, or an exception, or in some implementations an infinite loop. One NaN can corrupt the order of a million good values. Take an average of that list, and the NaN spreads: any arithmetic involving NaN produces NaN, so your sum is NaN, your average is NaN, one bad value has swallowed the entire result. Try to find a NaN in a set by searching for it, and you can't, because it isn't equal to itself, so the lookup never matches.

A value that isn't equal to itself sounds like a philosophy riddle. It's actually a careful decision about how to represent "we don't know," and the price of that decision is that one undefined result, left unchecked, can quietly wreck everything it touches.
