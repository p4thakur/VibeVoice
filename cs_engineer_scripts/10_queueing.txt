Push a server from 90 percent busy to 99 percent busy, and its response time doesn't get a little worse, it gets about eleven times worse. The server isn't broken. It's obeying a law of queues that governs checkout lines, traffic jams, and every system that processes requests. Once you know this law, you'll understand why "just add a bit more load, we have spare capacity" is one of the most dangerous sentences in operations.

Start with the intuition from a real queue, the supermarket. If a checkout is idle half the time, you walk right up, no wait. If it's busy almost every minute of the day, then the moment you arrive there's probably already a line, and that line takes time to clear before you're served. The fuller the server, the longer the wait, and the relationship is not a straight line. It curves, gently at first, then viciously upward.

Here's the law, in the simplest standard model of a queue. The average time your request spends waiting, measured in units of how long one request takes to actually serve, is roughly utilization divided by one-minus-utilization. Utilization is just the fraction of time the server is busy. Let's plug in numbers, because the numbers are the whole story. At 50 percent busy, the wait is 0.5 over 0.5, which is 1, you wait about as long as one request takes to serve. At 80 percent, it's 0.8 over 0.2, which is 4. At 90 percent, 0.9 over 0.1, which is 9. At 95 percent, it's 19. At 99 percent, it's 0.99 over 0.01, which is 99.

Look at the jump. Going from 90 to 99 percent utilization, you're only squeezing 9 percent more work out of the server, but the wait time goes from 9 units to 99 units, roughly eleven times worse. As utilization approaches 100 percent, that one-minus-utilization on the bottom approaches zero, and dividing by nearly-zero sends the wait toward infinity. The queue grows faster than you can drain it, and latency runs away.

Why does it blow up, rather than rising smoothly? Because requests don't arrive in a neat, even rhythm, they clump, randomly. When a server has idle time, a clump gets absorbed, the backlog clears before the next clump hits. But a nearly-full server has no idle time to absorb a clump, so every random burst adds to a line that never fully drains, and the waits compound. The randomness is essential: if work arrived perfectly evenly, you could run at 100 percent. It's the variability, combined with high utilization, that kills you.

This is why experienced engineers never plan to run their servers near full. That headroom that looks like waste, a fleet sitting at 40 or 50 percent, isn't waste, it's what keeps latency low and gives you room to absorb spikes and failures. When one server dies and its traffic shifts to the others, you need them to have slack, or the survivors cross into the runaway zone and you get a cascading collapse: each overloaded server slows, queues back up, and the whole system falls over, the same shape of failure behind many real outages.

It also reframes a tradeoff you'll face constantly. Running hot saves money on machines but risks latency and collapse. Running cool costs more machines but stays fast and resilient. Where you sit on that curve is a real decision, and this law is how you reason about it. If someone says a service is at 85 percent and wants to add 10 percent more traffic, you now know that's not a 10 percent change, it's a move up the steep part of the curve.

The same math, by the way, is why adding one more car to a nearly-full highway can trigger a jam that ripples backward for miles. Utilization near the limit plus natural variability equals runaway waiting. Fifty percent busy, wait of one. Ninety-nine percent busy, wait of ninety-nine. Leave the headroom, because the cliff is closer than it looks.
