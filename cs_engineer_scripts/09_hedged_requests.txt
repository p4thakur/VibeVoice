Google cut its worst-case latency by a factor of twenty, not by making anything faster, but by being willing to do the same work twice and throw one copy away. The technique is called hedged requests, and it's the most counterintuitive performance trick in distributed systems: sometimes the way to go faster is to waste a little work on purpose.

Here's the problem it attacks, and it follows straight from the tail-at-scale idea. When you send a request to a server, almost always it's quick. But every so often that one server is having a bad moment, a pause to clean up memory, a queue backed up behind someone else's heavy query, a slow disk read, and that one request takes 50 times longer than normal. You can't predict which request will be the unlucky one. And when your system waits on many servers, these rare slow responses dominate what users feel. The question is: how do you protect against a straggler when you don't know in advance which request will straggle?

The naive answer is to send every request to two servers and take the faster reply. That would work, but it doubles your traffic, and double the load makes every server slower, which can make the whole problem worse. Too expensive.

Hedging is the clever version. You send the request to one server and start waiting. You don't send a second copy immediately. You wait a short, specific amount of time, until the request has taken longer than, say, 95 percent of requests normally take. For 95 percent of requests, the answer comes back before that threshold and you never send a second copy at all. But for the slow 5 percent, the ones that cross the threshold, you now send a duplicate to a different server, and take whichever of the two answers first. The key insight: a request that's already slow is exactly the one likely to keep being slow, so that's precisely when a backup pays off, and because you only hedge the slow tail, you only add about 5 percent more traffic.

The numbers from Google's own systems are striking. In one example from their BigTable storage layer, sending a hedged request after a 10-millisecond delay reduced the 99.9th-percentile latency, the near-worst case, from 1,800 milliseconds down to 74 milliseconds, while adding only about 2 percent more requests. A near-twentyfold improvement at the bad tail, for a couple of percent of extra work. That's the trade: a tiny, controlled amount of wasted effort buys an enormous reduction in worst-case latency.

There's a refinement that cuts the waste even further, called tied requests. You send both copies, but each one carries a note telling it which other server also has the job. The instant one server actually starts working on it, it tells the other to drop it. So you almost never pay for the work twice, only for the brief window before one picks it up. Google used this to push the savings further still.

The reason this matters to you as an engineer isn't that you'll reimplement Google's storage layer. It's the mental shift. We're trained to think redundant work is pure waste. Hedging shows that in a world of unpredictable stragglers, a small amount of redundancy is the cheapest insurance you can buy against the tail, and the skill is in bounding it: hedge only the slow ones, cancel duplicates fast, and your extra cost stays tiny while your worst case collapses. You see the same instinct in systems that retry a slow read on a replica, or query two data centers and take the first answer.

Do the work twice, but only for the unlucky few, and only when they're already late. A couple of percent more traffic, and your worst-case latency drops twentyfold. That's hedging: spending a little waste to buy a lot of certainty.
