There's a rule in distributed systems that everyone quotes and most people quote wrong. It's called the CAP theorem, and the popular version, "pick two out of three: consistency, availability, partition tolerance," is misleading in a way that leads engineers to make the wrong choice. The accurate version is sharper, and it tells you something real about every distributed database you'll ever use.

First, the three words. Consistency here means every read sees the most recent write, everyone sees the same, current data, no matter which server they ask. Availability means every request gets a response, the system stays up and answers. Partition tolerance means the system keeps working even when the network between its servers fails, when messages between nodes are lost or delayed so that some servers can't talk to others.

Now, why "pick two of three" is wrong. It suggests partition tolerance is one option you might trade away, like choosing not to have it. But you don't get to opt out of network partitions. In any system whose data lives on more than one machine, the network will, eventually, fail, cables get cut, switches die, data centers lose their link. A partition is not a design choice, it's a fact of life that will happen to you. So "give up partition tolerance" isn't a real option, unless your system runs on a single machine, in which case it's not distributed and CAP doesn't apply.

So the honest statement is this: when a partition is happening, and sooner or later one will be, you must choose between consistency and availability. You can't have both during a partition. That's the whole theorem, and it's a choice forced on you only in the moment the network is broken, not all the time.

Here's why the choice is forced, concretely. Imagine your data is on two servers, and the link between them breaks. A write arrives at server A. Now A faces a dilemma. It can't reach B to tell it about the write. Option one: A accepts the write anyway and answers the user. Now A has new data that B doesn't, so anyone reading from B sees stale data, you've given up consistency to stay available. Option two: A refuses to accept the write until it can reach B and confirm, so that nobody ever sees inconsistent data, which means A returns an error or hangs, you've given up availability to stay consistent. There is no third door. Either you serve possibly-stale or divergent data, or you stop serving. The broken network removed the possibility of being both correct and up.

This is exactly the choice behind the split-brain defenses we've seen: the minority side of a partition shuts itself down, sacrificing availability, specifically to preserve consistency and avoid two diverging copies. That's a system choosing the C side of CAP. A different system, like a shopping cart that must always accept writes, chooses the A side: stay up, accept everything, and reconcile conflicts later, which is how Amazon's Dynamo was designed. Neither is universally right. It depends on what your application can tolerate. A bank balance leans toward consistency, refuse rather than show wrong money. A social feed or a shopping cart leans toward availability, stay up, sort out small conflicts afterward.

And there's a refinement worth knowing, because the basic theorem only talks about the moment of partition, which is rare. An extended version called PACELC adds: else, when the system is running normally with no partition, you still face a tradeoff between latency and consistency. Keeping all copies perfectly in sync on every write costs time, coordination between servers, so even in the healthy case, many systems relax consistency a little to go faster. So the real, everyday tradeoff is broader than CAP alone: during a partition it's consistency versus availability, and the rest of the time it's consistency versus latency.

Not "pick two of three." Partitions aren't optional, so the real choice is the one the broken network forces on you: correct, or available. Decide that on purpose, before the cable gets cut.
