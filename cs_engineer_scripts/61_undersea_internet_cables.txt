When you load a website hosted on another continent, your data almost certainly travels there through a physical cable lying on the floor of the ocean, a cable often no thicker than a garden hose, carrying the traffic of entire nations. Not satellites. Not some abstract cloud. Glass fibres under thousands of meters of seawater carry around 99 percent of all intercontinental internet traffic. The internet, for all its talk of clouds and wireless, is a profoundly physical thing, and understanding why we use cables instead of satellites teaches you a hard truth about the limits of speed.

Start with the surprising fact and the obvious question. There are hundreds of these submarine cables crisscrossing the oceans, forming the backbone that connects continents. And the question almost everyone asks is: in the age of satellites, why are we still running cables across the sea floor? Satellites are up there already. Why dig trenches in the ocean? The answer comes down to two unforgiving physical quantities: latency and bandwidth.

Take latency first, the delay for data to make a round trip, and it's governed by the speed of light, which is fast but finite. The communication satellites that long handled such links sit in a high orbit about 36,000 kilometers up. For your data to go up to such a satellite and back down, that's 72,000 kilometers just for one hop up and down, and a round trip doubles it. Even at light speed, that imposes a delay of roughly half a second before anything else. Half a second of lag on every exchange makes interactive things, a video call, a web page with dozens of requests, a game, feel sluggish or broken. An undersea cable, by contrast, runs more or less directly between two coasts. The distance is far shorter than a trip to high orbit and back, so the light-speed delay is a small fraction of the satellite's. For responsiveness, the short physical path of a cable wins decisively.

Then bandwidth, how much data per second. A single modern undersea cable bundles many fibre-optic strands, and each strand carries enormous amounts of data by sending multiple colors of laser light down the glass at once, each color an independent channel. A single cable can carry hundreds of terabits per second, the combined traffic of millions of people. Satellites, sharing limited radio spectrum beamed across huge distances, carry vastly less, and that capacity is split among everyone in the satellite's footprint. So for sheer volume, a cable isn't a little better than a satellite, it's in a completely different league, orders of magnitude more.

The physics of the cable itself is quietly impressive. Light sent down a glass fibre weakens over distance, so the cables include repeaters every so often along the sea floor, devices that boost the signal so it can cross an entire ocean. Those repeaters need power, so the cable also carries electricity along its length to run them, fed from the shore stations at each end. A transoceanic cable is thus not a passive wire but a powered, amplified system lying in the dark miles underwater, maintained by a small fleet of specialized repair ships.

And that physicality is also a vulnerability, which is the sobering part. These cables can be, and regularly are, damaged, by ship anchors dragged across them, by fishing trawlers, by undersea landslides, and occasionally by deliberate sabotage. When a major cable is cut, traffic has to reroute through others, which can slow or disrupt connectivity for whole regions until repair ships physically locate the break, haul the cable up from the sea floor, and splice it, which can take days or weeks. A surprising amount of the world's connectivity depends on a relatively small number of these cables and the chokepoints they pass through.

Ninety-nine percent of the traffic between continents runs through hoses of glass on the ocean floor, not through the sky. Because the speed of light is a hard limit, and a short path under the sea beats a long one to orbit, every single time. The internet is not a cloud. It's cables, and someone has to go out and fix them when an anchor cuts one.
