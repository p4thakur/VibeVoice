On the night of January 31st, 2017, a tired GitLab engineer, fighting a system problem, typed a command to wipe an empty database directory so he could rebuild it. A second later he realized, with horror, that he'd run it on the wrong server, the live production database, and was watching 300 gigabytes of real user data disappear. He killed the command, but only about 4.5 gigabytes were left. Then came the gut-punch that makes this story legendary: they went to restore from backup, and discovered that not one of their five separate backup methods actually worked. This is the GitLab incident, and it's the most honest lesson in computing about the difference between having backups and having backups that work.

Start with the setup, because the mistake is one every engineer fears. GitLab was having database trouble that night, replication lag, some spam cleanup gone sideways. The engineer was trying to fix a struggling replica by wiping its data directory and resyncing it fresh. He meant to run the delete on the broken secondary server. But working late across multiple terminal windows, he ran it on the primary, the live one. By the time he noticed the command was deleting far too much and stopped it, almost the entire production database was gone. A single command, the wrong window, and the data behind a service millions relied on was vanishing in real time. That part is just the human setup. The real story is what happened next.

They reached for their backups, and here is the sequence that should be burned into every engineer's memory, because they had five layers of protection and all five failed. They had regular database backups taken with a standard tool, but a version mismatch meant those backups were silently producing almost nothing, empty files, and no one had noticed because no one checked. They had disk snapshots, but they weren't enabled for that database server. They had replication, but that's what was broken and had just been wiped. They had another backup process uploading to cloud storage, but it wasn't working either. Backup after backup, each one assumed to be there, each one revealed, in the emergency, to be absent or broken. Five safety nets, five holes.

What finally saved them was luck, not design. One engineer, hours before, had happened to take a manual snapshot of the database to a staging server, for an unrelated reason. That accidental copy was roughly six hours old. So GitLab restored from it, and lost about six hours of data, the issues, comments, merge requests, and accounts created in that window were gone forever, though the actual code repositories, stored separately, were safe. Six hours of loss instead of total loss, and only because of a side copy nobody had planned as a backup. And then GitLab did something remarkable: they live-streamed the entire recovery and published a brutally honest postmortem, which is why we can all learn from it.

There are supporting lessons too, each real. Dangerous, irreversible commands should have guardrails, confirmations, or restricted access, so that one tired keystroke on the wrong server can't erase production, the same lesson as giving loop-driven systems a kill switch. Production systems should be clearly distinguished from staging so you can't confuse the windows. And the whole thing is a reminder that fatigue and pressure are when these mistakes happen, so the system, not the exhausted human, must be the thing that prevents catastrophe.

Three hundred gigabytes deleted by one command on the wrong server, and five backup methods that all turned out to be broken. Saved only by an accidental copy someone happened to make. A backup you've never restored isn't a backup. Go test yours, today, before the night you can't afford to find out.
