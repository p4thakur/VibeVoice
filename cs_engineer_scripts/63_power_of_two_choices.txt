Here's a result that feels like it shouldn't be true. If you're spreading work across servers and you assign each job to a random server, some servers end up badly overloaded. But if, for each job, you pick two servers at random and send it to the less busy of the two, the worst overload almost vanishes. Just looking at two instead of one changes everything. It's called the power of two choices, and it's one of the highest-leverage ideas in load balancing.

Start with the pure random approach, because it's what most people reach for and it's worse than it looks. You have a job, you pick one server uniformly at random, you send it there. On average, every server gets the same amount of work, which sounds fine. But "on average" hides the problem, which is the imbalance, the gap between the average server and the unluckiest, most-overloaded one. With pure random assignment, by chance some servers happen to receive far more than their share. If you throw n jobs at n servers at random, the busiest server ends up with roughly log n over log log n jobs, a quantity that grows as the system gets bigger. For a large cluster, the busiest server might hold five, six, seven times the average. And the busiest server is what determines your worst-case latency, the tail everyone waits on. So random balancing leaves you with a stubbornly lumpy load.

The obvious fix is to always pick the least busy server of all, check all of them, send the job to the emptiest. That gives perfect balance, but it's expensive: you have to query the load of every server for every job, which is a lot of communication, and in a fast-moving system that global information is stale the moment you gather it. In a big cluster, checking everyone for every request doesn't scale.

Now the magic. Instead of checking one server, or all of them, you check just two, chosen at random, and send the job to whichever of those two is less loaded. That's the entire change. And the effect on the worst-case load is not a small improvement, it's a dramatic one. The busiest server now holds only about log log n jobs above the average, instead of log n over log log n. To feel the difference: log of a million is about 14, but log log of a million is under 3. The imbalance collapses from growing-with-scale to essentially a small constant. With two choices, the busiest server is barely busier than the average, no matter how large the cluster gets.

Why does looking at one more option help so wildly? Because a single random choice is blind, it can always land on a server that's already overloaded, with no chance to notice. Adding just one alternative gives you the power to avoid the worse of two, and crucially, for a server to receive a job, it must be the lesser-loaded of a random pair, which becomes rapidly less likely the busier it already is. A heavily loaded server almost always loses the comparison, so it stops attracting new work. That gentle corrective force, applied on every single assignment with almost no extra cost, is enough to flatten the whole distribution. And going from two choices to three helps only a little more, nearly all the benefit comes from that first jump, from one to two. The hard part was having any choice at all.

For an engineer, the practical payoff is huge, because two choices is cheap. You don't need global state, you don't need to coordinate, you just sample two and compare, so it scales to enormous systems. This is why real load balancers and distributed queues use it, and why it shows up in the hashing strategies behind distributed caches to even out where keys land. NGINX and many others offer a "power of two choices" balancing mode for exactly this reason.

And the idea outlives the specific algorithm: a tiny bit of informed choice beats both blind luck and exhaustive perfection. One random pick is lumpy. Checking everyone doesn't scale. Checking two and taking the better one gets you almost all the balance, for almost none of the cost. In a lot of systems, two is the magic number.
