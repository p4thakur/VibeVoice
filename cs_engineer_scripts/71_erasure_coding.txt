Amazon S3 promises your data has eleven nines of durability, a 99.999999999 percent chance of surviving any given year. To store a file three times would be the obvious way to be safe, but it wastes two-thirds of your disk. The real systems get far better safety for far less overhead using an idea from deep space communication, called erasure coding, and its core trick is a piece of algebra you learned without realizing its power.

Start with the simple approach: replication. To protect a file, store three copies on three different machines. If one dies, you have two more. It works, and it's easy, but the cost is brutal: to store one petabyte of actual data, you buy three petabytes of disk. That's 200 percent overhead, two-thirds of your hardware exists only as backup. At the scale of Amazon or Facebook, that difference is billions of dollars. And it only protects you against a limited number of simultaneous failures for that cost.

Erasure coding asks a sharper question: can we survive several failures without paying for whole duplicate copies? And the answer comes from an idea as old as school algebra. Think about two points. Two points define a line. If I give you two points, you can draw the unique line through them, and from that line you can find any other point on it. Now, here's the key: if I give you three points that all lie on that same line, you could lose any one of them and still recover the line from the remaining two, and therefore recover the lost point. I gave you one point more than necessary, one unit of redundancy, and it let me lose one point. Extend the idea with higher-degree curves: a curve of the right degree is pinned down by a certain number of points, and any extra points you place on it are redundancy, lose a few, recover them from the rest.

That's exactly what erasure coding does with data, using a scheme called Reed-Solomon. You take your data and split it into, say, 10 chunks. Then you compute 4 extra chunks, called parity chunks, using that polynomial idea, so that all 14 chunks together describe one mathematical object, and any 10 of the 14 are enough to reconstruct the entire original. Spread those 14 chunks across 14 different machines or disks. Now you can lose any 4 of them, any 4, and still rebuild your data perfectly from the surviving 10.

Count the cost. You stored 14 chunks to protect 10 chunks of real data, so the overhead is 4 over 10, just 40 percent, versus replication's 200 percent. And yet you can survive 4 simultaneous failures, which is more protection than storing 3 full copies gives you, since 3 copies only survive 2 losses. Less than a quarter of the storage overhead, and more fault tolerance. That's the win, and it's why this math from deep-space probes, where you can't retransmit, and from CDs and QR codes, which keep working when scratched or smudged, underlies large-scale storage like S3, Facebook's cold storage, and Hadoop's file system.

Nothing is free, and the tradeoffs are the part a senior engineer names. First, reconstruction is expensive. With replication, if a disk dies you just copy a surviving replica, cheap. With erasure coding, to rebuild one lost chunk you must read 10 other chunks from 10 machines and do the math to recompute it. That's a lot of network traffic and CPU for every repair, which is why erasure coding is favored for data that's written once and rarely read or lost, like backups and cold storage, and replication is often kept for hot, frequently-accessed data where fast recovery matters. Second, updating a small piece of data means recomputing parity, so it's a poor fit for data that changes constantly. The property that makes it space-efficient, that all the chunks are mathematically entangled, is the same property that makes repairs and updates costly. One mechanism, two faces, again.

Ten chunks of data, four of parity, and you can lose any four and lose nothing. Not three whole copies, just enough cleverly-computed redundancy, borrowed from the algebra of points on a curve, to make eleven nines affordable.
