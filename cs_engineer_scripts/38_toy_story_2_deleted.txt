In 1998, during the making of Toy Story 2, someone at Pixar typed a command on the film's server, and the movie began deleting itself. Characters vanished, then whole scenes, as the command chewed through the files. They pulled the plug within seconds, but most of the film was already gone. And then they discovered the backups were broken too. Toy Story 2 survived only because one person happened to have a copy at home. It's a story almost identical in shape to GitLab's, a decade earlier, and together they hammer home the same unforgiving lesson about backups.

Start with how the film existed. A computer-animated movie isn't footage, it's a vast collection of files: the models of the characters, their rigging, the sets, the lighting, the animation data, all living on a shared file server that the whole team worked from. That server was the movie. And on Unix systems, there's a command that recursively deletes everything under a directory, the famous rm -rf, which removes files and everything inside them, without confirmation, without a trash can to recover from. Somehow, that command got run on the directory holding the film. As it executed, it walked through the files deleting them one by one, and people watching could literally see pieces of the movie disappearing, a character's model gone, then a sequence, as the deletion marched through. Someone had the presence of mind to physically unplug the machine, but by then roughly 90 percent of the film's files were gone.

No problem, you'd think, that's what backups are for. And Pixar had a backup system. But when they went to restore, they found the backups were failing, incomplete or not working properly, so they could not bring the film back from them. Exactly like GitLab, the safety net they were counting on turned out, in the moment of crisis, to have a hole in it. The movie, years of work by hundreds of people, was on the brink of being genuinely lost.

What saved Toy Story 2 was pure luck wearing the disguise of a side copy. One of the film's technical leads had recently had a baby and was working partly from home, and to do that, she had a copy of the movie's files on a computer at her house. That home copy, made for the mundane reason of working remotely, was now the only surviving near-complete version of the film. They carefully drove the machine back to the studio, treating it like precious cargo, and restored the movie from it. The film was recovered not because of any backup strategy, but because of an unplanned, accidental copy that existed for an unrelated reason, the same miracle that saved GitLab.

And the fact that it recurs, in a film studio in 1998 and a software company in 2017, is exactly why it's worth stating plainly: having a backup system is not the same as having working backups, and you only find out which you have when you try to restore. Pixar had backups. They didn't work when it mattered. The default state of a backup that nobody regularly tests by restoring from it is broken, because backups fail silently, and silence reads as success right up until the disaster. Both stories were rescued by accidental side copies, which is another way of saying both organizations' actual, intended safety nets failed completely, and luck filled the gap. You cannot plan to be lucky.

There are the supporting lessons too, and they echo GitLab's. A command as destructive and irreversible as rm -rf, run against critical data, should never be that easy to fire without guardrails, confirmations, permissions, separation between a person's reach and the one copy of something irreplaceable. The single live copy of years of work should not be one typo away from deletion.

A classic film, 90 percent deleted in seconds, with broken backups, rescued only because someone happened to have it on a home computer. It happened to Pixar in 1998 and to GitLab in 2017 in almost the same way. The backup isn't the copy. The backup is the restore you've actually tested. Go test yours.
