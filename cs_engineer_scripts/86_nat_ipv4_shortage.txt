There are about 8 billion people and tens of billions of connected devices, but the address system the internet was built on, IPv4, has only about 4.3 billion addresses total. We ran out of them years ago. And yet everything still works, your phone, your laptop, your smart TV, all online at once. The reason the internet didn't grind to a halt is a clever workaround called NAT, and understanding it explains a lot of puzzling things, like why hosting a game server from home is such a pain.

Start with the hard limit. An IPv4 address is a 32-bit number. Two to the power of 32 is about 4.3 billion, so that's the total count of possible addresses, ever. In the 1980s, when the internet was a research project, 4.3 billion seemed limitless. But as the whole world came online, each with multiple devices, demand blew past supply. By 2011, the central pool of unassigned addresses was officially exhausted. There were simply not enough public addresses to give every device its own.

The workaround that saved the day is Network Address Translation, NAT, and the core idea is sharing one public address among many devices. Here's how it works in your home. Your internet provider gives your home router a single public address, the one the outside internet sees. Inside your home, every device, your phone, laptop, TV, gets a private address, one of those addresses like 192.168-something that aren't valid on the public internet and are reused in millions of homes simultaneously. These private addresses are purely local; the outside world never sees them and they don't have to be unique globally, which is exactly how we sidestep the shortage.

Now the translation, which is the actual mechanism. When your phone, with its private address, sends a request to a website, the packet hits your router. The router rewrites the packet: it replaces your phone's private address with the router's single public address, so to the website, the request appears to come from the router. But the router also records, in a table, "this particular conversation belongs to the phone." When the website replies to the router's public address, the router consults its table, sees the reply belongs to the phone, rewrites the packet to the phone's private address, and delivers it inside. The router is a translator sitting at the boundary, swapping private for public on the way out and public for private on the way back.

The piece that makes one address serve many devices at once is port numbers. Every connection uses a port number as a sort of sub-address, and there are about 65,000 of them available. So the router can juggle thousands of simultaneous conversations through its one public address by assigning each a different port and remembering which device each port belongs to. One public address, tens of thousands of possible concurrent conversations. That's how a whole household, or with a technique called carrier-grade NAT, even hundreds of separate customers, can share a single public IPv4 address.

But NAT has a real cost, and it's the thing that explains those everyday frustrations. Because your devices hide behind the router with private addresses, the outside world cannot directly start a connection to them. Your phone can reach out, and the router remembers the conversation so replies get back, but a random computer on the internet has no way to initiate a connection to your phone, there's no public address that points to it, and the router wouldn't know which inside device to route an unexpected incoming connection to. This breaks the internet's original design, where every device was directly reachable. It's why hosting a game server or running a service from home requires port forwarding, manually telling the router "incoming connections on this port go to that device." It's why video calls and peer-to-peer apps need clever tricks to establish a direct connection between two devices that are both hidden behind NATs.

The permanent fix is IPv6, which uses 128-bit addresses, giving a number so vast, over 340 undecillion, that every device on Earth could have billions of its own, with no sharing and no NAT needed. IPv6 has been rolling out for over two decades, slowly, because the internet is too big to change overnight, and NAT worked well enough to remove the urgency. So we live in a long transition, with NAT quietly buying time.

Four billion addresses, tens of billions of devices, and the internet still runs. Not because we found more addresses, but because we learned to share one among a whole household, by rewriting packets and remembering who asked for what.
