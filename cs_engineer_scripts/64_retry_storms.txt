A service gets a little slow. Every client politely retries. And that retry, the most reasonable reaction in the world, is what turns a small hiccup into a total outage. It's called a retry storm, and the math behind it, how a well-meaning retry multiplies load exactly when the system can least afford it, is something every engineer who builds networked services needs burned into their brain.

Start with the innocent beginning. A service is running near its limit, and something small happens, a brief spike, a slow database query, and some requests start taking too long or failing. The clients calling that service are built to be resilient, so when a request fails, they retry it, maybe up to three times. Sensible. A single dropped request shouldn't fail the whole operation.

But look at what that does to load. The service was already struggling under normal traffic. Now every failed request comes back two or three more times. If a third of requests are failing and each failure retries three times, you've suddenly multiplied the traffic hitting an already-overloaded service. The extra load makes it slower still, which makes more requests fail, which triggers more retries, which adds more load. This is a feedback loop, and it runs the wrong way: the worse things get, the more traffic the clients generate, which makes things worse. The system can't recover, because recovery requires load to drop, and the retries guarantee load climbs.

Now add the truly frightening part: layers. Real systems are stacks of services calling services. Say your web tier calls a middle service, which calls a database, and each layer retries three times on failure. When the database gets slow, the middle service retries it three times. But the web tier is also retrying the middle service three times, and each of those attempts triggers the middle service's three retries to the database. Three times three is nine. Add another layer and it's three times three times three, twenty-seven. A single original request from a user can become twenty-seven hammer-blows at the struggling database at the bottom. The retries multiply through every layer, so the deepest, most overloaded component gets the most amplified traffic. Teams have watched a minor blip at the database turn into a complete collapse this way, and the access logs show the bottom service receiving many times the real request volume.

So how do you let clients be resilient without building a doomsday machine? Several defenses, and they all aim at breaking the feedback loop. First, exponential backoff: don't retry immediately, wait a bit, and double the wait each time, so a struggling service gets a breather instead of an instant second punch. Second, and people forget this one, add jitter, randomness, to the backoff. Without jitter, all the failed clients wait the same amount and then retry at the same instant, a synchronized wave that slams the service in lockstep. Jitter smears the retries out over time so they arrive spread out, not as a thundering herd. Third, the circuit breaker: if a service keeps failing, the client stops calling it entirely for a while, "the circuit is open," giving it room to recover instead of piling on, then cautiously tries again later. Fourth, retry budgets: cap retries so they can only ever add, say, 10 percent more traffic across the system, never 3x or 27x.

A small slowdown, met with sensible retries, three layers deep, becomes twenty-seven times the load on the weakest point, exactly when it's already drowning. The fix isn't to stop retrying. It's to retry in a way that backs off, spreads out, and knows when to stop.
