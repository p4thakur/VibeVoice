For over a century, mathematicians believed multiplying two numbers couldn't be done faster than the way you learned in school. One of the greatest mathematicians of the age said so out loud. Then a 23-year-old, in a week, proved him wrong. The method is called Karatsuba multiplication, and the trick it uses, buying speed by doing one clever subtraction to avoid one expensive multiplication, is the heart of divide-and-conquer thinking.

Start with the schoolbook method, because the cost is the point. To multiply two numbers, you multiply every digit of one by every digit of the other, then add up the results. If each number has n digits, that's n times n, or n squared, single-digit multiplications. For two 2-digit numbers, 4 multiplications. For two 1000-digit numbers, a million. Double the length of the numbers, and the work quadruples. For the gigantic numbers used in cryptography, hundreds or thousands of digits long, n squared is punishing.

In 1960, the famous mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov conjectured that n squared was essentially the best anyone could ever do, that multiplication fundamentally required that much work. He said so in a seminar. One of the students in the room was Anatoly Karatsuba. Within about a week, Karatsuba found a method that beat it, and the story goes that Kolmogorov was so struck he wrote it up and published it under Karatsuba's name.

Here's the idea. Take two large numbers and split each one in half, into a high part and a low part. Call the first number's halves a and b, and the second's halves c and d. The full product, when you expand it, needs four smaller products: a times c, a times d, b times c, and b times d. Four multiplications of half-size numbers. So far that's no better, splitting and doing four half-size multiplications works out to the same n squared.

Karatsuba's insight was that you don't need all four. Look at the middle: you need a times d plus b times c, but you don't actually need those two products separately, you only need their sum. So compute just one extra product: a plus b, times c plus d. Multiply that out and it equals a·c plus a·d plus b·c plus b·d. Now subtract the two products you already have, a·c and b·d, and what's left is exactly a·d plus b·c, the middle sum you wanted. So instead of four half-size multiplications, you do three: a·c, b·d, and that one combined product, recovering the middle with a couple of cheap subtractions and additions. Addition and subtraction are far cheaper than multiplication, so trading one multiplication for a few additions is a winning deal.

Three instead of four doesn't sound dramatic, but it compounds, because you apply the same trick recursively: each of those three half-size multiplications is itself done by splitting into three quarter-size ones, and so on all the way down. Doing three subproblems at half size each time, instead of four, changes the growth from n squared to roughly n to the power 1.585. For two numbers a thousand digits long, that's the difference between about a million basic multiplications and roughly fifty thousand. The bigger the numbers, the wider the gap.

The reason this matters to an engineer is twofold. First, it's why the big-number libraries underneath cryptography and scientific computing don't use schoolbook multiplication for large inputs, they switch to Karatsuba and its even faster descendants once the numbers are big enough. Second, and more lasting, it's the purest example of the divide-and-conquer principle that reshaped algorithms: splitting a problem is only half the move, the real win comes from reducing how many subproblems you create. The same realization, that you can cut the number of subproblems with a clever algebraic rearrangement, later gave us faster matrix multiplication and the fast Fourier transform that powers signal processing.

A conjecture from a legend, overturned in a week by a student, by noticing that four multiplications could be three. The point isn't multiplying. It's that even the operations everyone assumes are as fast as they can be sometimes aren't, if you're willing to trade an expensive step for a few cheap ones.
