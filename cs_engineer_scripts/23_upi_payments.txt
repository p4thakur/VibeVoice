India runs a payment system that handles around 20 billion transactions a month, lets you pay anyone instantly with just a phone, and works across hundreds of different banks and apps that all speak to each other. It's called UPI, and whether you send money from one app or another, to one bank or another, it just works, in real time, for free. The design ideas that make that possible are worth understanding, because they're a masterclass in how a well-designed abstraction and a shared protocol beat a pile of private systems.

Start with the problem UPI solved. Before it, moving money between banks was slow and clumsy. To pay someone you needed their account number and bank code, transfers took hours or a day, and every bank was an island with its own app and rules. If you banked at one place and your friend at another, paying them was a chore. The core problem is interoperability: how do you make hundreds of separate banks, each with their own systems, able to send money to each other instantly, so that a user never has to care which bank or app anyone is using?

UPI's answer was to build a shared switch in the middle, run by a central body, that all banks connect to. Think of it as a universal translator and router sitting between every bank. When you pay someone, your app talks to this central switch, the switch figures out which banks are involved, routes the request between them, and coordinates the transfer. Every bank implements the same standard protocol to talk to the switch, so any bank can transact with any other through this common hub, instead of every bank having to build a private connection to every other bank, which would be an impossible tangle. One shared standard in the middle replaces a mess of point-to-point links, the same reason the internet itself uses common protocols.

The second brilliant idea is an abstraction that hides your bank details, called the virtual payment address. Instead of handing out your account number, you get a simple handle, something like a name at a provider. When someone sends money to that handle, the system translates it, behind the scenes, into your actual bank account. This layer of indirection, the same powerful trick we saw in virtual memory, decouples your public identity from your private bank details. You can point your handle at a different bank account without anyone who pays you needing to know or change anything. Your account number stays secret, and the thing people use to pay you is a stable, simple name. The abstraction hides the messy underlying detail and makes the whole thing feel effortless.

Now the engineering challenge of scale, because 20 billion transactions a month is roughly 7,000 to 8,000 per second on average, and far more at peaks like festival days. Every one of those is real money, so every one must be handled with absolute correctness, no double-spending, no lost transfers, even when the network hiccups. This is exactly where the idempotency idea becomes essential. If your payment request gets a weak signal and your phone retries, the system must ensure the money moves only once, recognizing the retry as the same transaction rather than a second payment, the precise problem idempotency keys were built to solve. At this scale, with money on the line, making every operation safe to retry isn't a nicety, it's the foundation.

And doing it in real time adds pressure. Traditional bank transfers could be slow because they settled in batches. UPI resolves each payment in seconds, which means the central switch and the banks must confirm and commit a transaction on the spot, coordinating between the sender's bank and the receiver's bank, while staying consistent, the money must leave one account and arrive in the other as one atomic outcome, never half-done. That's the same all-or-nothing guarantee that the write-ahead log and transactions provide, applied across institutions.

Twenty billion payments a month, any app to any app, any bank to any bank, instant and free. Not because one company built one giant system, but because everyone agreed on a shared protocol in the middle, and a simple handle hid the complexity underneath.
