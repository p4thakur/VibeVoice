TCP, the system that carries almost all your internet traffic, deliberately starts every connection slow, sending just a trickle of data and doubling its speed only gradually. It feels like a flaw, why not just blast data at full speed immediately? But this cautious ramp-up is one of the most important things keeping the entire internet from collapsing, and the story of why reveals a disaster most people have never heard of.

Start with the problem TCP faces at the start of every connection. When your computer begins sending data to a server, it has no idea how fast the path between them can handle. Is it a fat fibre line that can take gigabits, or a weak mobile link that can barely manage a trickle? There's no sign posted. And the links in between are shared with everyone else's traffic. So here's the danger: if your computer just sent data as fast as it possibly could from the very first moment, and the path couldn't handle it, the excess packets would pile up in the buffers of routers along the way, overflow, and get dropped. Worse, everyone else sharing those links would suffer the same, and the network chokes.

This isn't hypothetical. In the mid-1980s, the early internet suffered exactly this, an event called congestion collapse. Everyone's machines sent as fast as they wanted, the shared links jammed, packets were dropped, dropped packets were resent, which added even more traffic to an already-jammed network, and throughput fell off a cliff. At one point a link that should have run fast slowed to a tiny fraction of its capacity, because it was drowning in retransmissions of packets that kept getting dropped. The network was effectively eating itself. An engineer named Van Jacobson devised the fix, a set of rules TCP still uses today, and the first of them is slow start.

Here's how slow start works. TCP keeps a number called the congestion window, which is the amount of data it's allowed to have "in flight," sent but not yet confirmed as received. It starts this window small, today typically about 10 packets, roughly 14 kilobytes. It sends that little bit, then waits for acknowledgments to come back confirming they arrived. Each acknowledgment is a signal that says "the path handled that fine, there's room for more." So for each one that returns, TCP enlarges the window, and the effect is that the window roughly doubles every round trip: 10 packets, then 20, then 40, then 80, climbing exponentially. It probes the path's capacity by steadily, rapidly increasing its sending rate, rather than guessing and overwhelming it.

This continues until the first sign of trouble, a lost packet, which TCP reads as "the path is full, I pushed too hard." At that point it backs off sharply, cutting its rate roughly in half, and then grows more gently from there, nudging upward one step at a time instead of doubling. So the overall rhythm is: ramp up fast to find the limit, then back off hard and probe carefully around it. Cautious increase, sharp decrease, forever feeling out how much the network can take without tipping it over. Every TCP connection on earth is politely testing the waters like this, which is collectively what keeps shared links from collapsing.

Now the consequence that matters for anyone building web services. Because connections start slow and ramp up, a brand-new connection is slow for its first moments. Reaching a window of, say, a megabyte of data in flight takes several round trips of doubling, and if each round trip is 100 milliseconds, that's half a second or more before the connection is running at full speed. For a small web page or a quick API call, the entire transfer might finish while the connection is still in its cautious ramp-up, never reaching full speed at all. This is why, for many websites, latency, the round-trip time, matters more than raw bandwidth: a connection to a distant server takes more round trips to speed up, so it spends more of its short life crawling.

And this explains a pile of real engineering practices. It's why content delivery networks put servers physically close to users, shorter round trips mean the window doubles faster, so the connection reaches full speed sooner. It's why reusing an existing connection is so much faster than opening a fresh one, the old one has already ramped up, while a new one starts from the slow trickle again. And it's why newer congestion-control algorithms, like Google's BBR, try to estimate the path's actual capacity directly rather than waiting to lose a packet, aiming to ramp up smarter.

TCP crawls at the start of every connection on purpose, because the alternative, everyone blasting full speed into shared pipes, once brought the early internet to its knees. Slow on purpose, so the network stays fast for everyone.
