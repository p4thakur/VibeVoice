When you open Instagram or Twitter, your feed loads instantly, pulling together posts from hundreds of people you follow, sorted by time. Doing that the obvious way, the moment you open the app, is far too slow to work. So the big social networks do something that sounds wasteful and is actually brilliant: they build your feed before you ask for it. The design is called fan-out, and the hard part, the part they solve with a hybrid, is a single celebrity.

First, the two ways to build a feed, because the whole story is a choice between them. Imagine the naive approach, called fan-out on read. When you open the app, the system goes to every one of the, say, 500 people you follow, grabs their recent posts, merges them all together, sorts by time, and shows you the top ones. The problem: that's a huge amount of work done at the exact moment you're staring at a loading spinner. Merging 500 people's posts, sorted, on demand, for every user every time they refresh, is enormous and slow. Reads happen constantly, so you'd be doing this expensive merge constantly.

The opposite approach is fan-out on write. The insight is that people read their feed far more often than the people they follow post. So do the work when someone posts, not when someone reads. The moment I publish a post, the system looks up my followers and immediately inserts my post into each of their personal feed lists, which are kept ready in a fast store like Redis. So every user has a pre-built timeline sitting in memory, already merged, already sorted. When you open the app, there's almost no work to do: just read your ready-made list. The expensive merging got moved from read time, which is frequent and latency-sensitive, to write time, which is rarer and can happen in the background. You've shifted the cost to where it hurts least.

For a normal user, this is perfect. I post, and it gets copied into my 500 followers' feeds, 500 quick writes, done in the background, nobody's waiting. But now meet the problem that breaks it: the celebrity. Suppose someone has 100 million followers. They post once. Fan-out on write now means 100 million inserts, into 100 million feeds, for a single post. If a few celebrities post around the same time, the system drowns in hundreds of millions of writes. The very approach that makes normal feeds fast becomes a catastrophe for the biggest accounts. This is sometimes called the celebrity problem or the hot-user problem.

So the real systems use a hybrid, and this is the part worth remembering for a design interview. For ordinary users, fan-out on write, copy their posts into followers' feeds ahead of time, cheap and fast. But for the handful of celebrity accounts with enormous followings, don't fan out on write at all, that would be millions of writes per post. Instead, leave their posts in one place, and fan out on read just for them: when you open your app, the system reads your pre-built feed of normal accounts, and separately grabs the latest posts from the few celebrities you follow, and merges those in at the last moment. Because you only follow a small number of celebrities, that last-moment merge is tiny. You get the best of both: the pre-built speed for the thousands of normal accounts, and the write-saving of not copying a celebrity's post a hundred million times.

The deeper lesson, and it shows up all over system design, is that there's no single right answer to "do the work early or do the work late." It depends on the ratio of reads to writes, and on the distribution. Most users are cheap to fan out on write, so you do. A few users are catastrophically expensive, so you handle them the opposite way. Recognizing that a system has two very different populations, and refusing to serve them both with one strategy, is exactly the kind of thinking that separates a design that works in a demo from one that survives a celebrity joining.

Build the feed before it's asked for, except for the one account whose post would need a hundred million copies. Precompute the common case, handle the extreme case the other way, and merge them at the door.
