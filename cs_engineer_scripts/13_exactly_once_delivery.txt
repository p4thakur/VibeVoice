"Exactly-once delivery" is the thing everyone wants from a messaging system, and it's the thing that, taken literally, is impossible. Yet systems like Kafka advertise exactly-once and they're not lying. Understanding how both things are true, that it's impossible and that you can still get it, is one of the real dividing lines between a junior and a senior distributed-systems engineer.

Start with why true exactly-once delivery is impossible, and it's the same wall we hit with payments. A sender ships a message to a receiver and waits for an acknowledgment. If the acknowledgment doesn't come back, the sender faces an unanswerable question: did the receiver never get the message, or did it get the message and process it, but the acknowledgment was lost on the way back? These two situations look identical to the sender, there is no possible way to tell them apart from the outside. So the sender must choose a policy, and there are only two honest choices.

Choice one: if unsure, don't resend. Then every message is sent at most once, but if an acknowledgment ever gets lost, you might have dropped a message that actually never arrived. That's at-most-once: no duplicates, but possible loss. Choice two: if unsure, resend. Then you never lose a message, but if the original actually did arrive and only the acknowledgment was lost, you've now delivered it twice. That's at-least-once: no loss, but possible duplicates. Those are your only real options for delivery over an unreliable network. You cannot have a guarantee that the message lands on the other side one time and exactly one time, because the network can always hide whether a step happened.

This connects to a classic puzzle called the Two Generals Problem. Two generals on opposite hills must attack at the same time, and can only communicate by messengers who might be captured. They can never become perfectly certain they agree, because every message needs a confirmation, and that confirmation needs a confirmation, and so on forever. Agreement over an unreliable channel, with certainty, is provably unreachable. Delivery is the same shape of problem.

So how does Kafka claim exactly-once? Here's the sleight of hand, and it's a brilliant one. You give up on exactly-once delivery, and you aim for exactly-once processing, which is a different and achievable thing. The trick is to combine the safe delivery policy, at-least-once, so you never lose anything, with a receiver that recognizes and discards duplicates. You deliver at least once, possibly twice, and then you make the second delivery harmless.

How does the receiver recognize a duplicate? The same idea as idempotency keys: every message carries a unique identifier. The receiver keeps track of which identifiers it has already processed. When a message arrives, it checks: have I handled this ID before? If yes, it throws the duplicate away and just re-sends the acknowledgment. If no, it processes it and records the ID. The message may be delivered twice, but it only ever takes effect once. People call the result effectively-once, and it's what "exactly-once" always means in practice, under the hood it's at-least-once delivery plus deduplication, or operations built to be idempotent so that reprocessing changes nothing.

The reason this matters to you isn't just vocabulary. It's that if a vendor promises literal exactly-once delivery with no conditions, you should be skeptical, because the network can't provide it. What good systems actually provide is at-least-once plus a deduplication or idempotency mechanism, and knowing that tells you what you're responsible for. If your message processing isn't idempotent, then even a system that deduplicates can hurt you when a duplicate slips through a boundary, so you design your handlers to be safe to run twice, the same lesson as before.

Impossible to deliver exactly once. Entirely possible to process exactly once. The senior move is to stop fighting the network for a guarantee it can't give, and instead make duplicates and retries harmless, so that "at least once, done safely" becomes, for all practical purposes, "exactly once."
