Amazon S3 promises your data has eleven nines of durability: 99.999999999 percent. Put a number on what that means and it's staggering, if you store ten million files, you'd expect to lose a single one roughly once every ten thousand years. How do you make storage that reliable, when it's built from ordinary disks that fail every day? The answer is that durability at this level isn't a property of the hardware, it's something engineered on top of hardware that's assumed to be constantly failing, and understanding it ties together several ideas we've built up.

Start by being clear what the promise is, because people confuse it with uptime. Durability means not losing your data, that once stored, it will still be there, intact, years later. That's different from availability, which is whether you can reach it at any given moment. Eleven nines is a durability claim, and it's about surviving the relentless death of the physical media underneath. And those media really do die, constantly. As we saw with disk failures at scale, when you run millions of drives, some are failing every hour, all day, forever. The individual disk is not reliable, its annual failure rate is a few percent. So how do you get eleven nines of durability from parts that fail that often?

The first pillar is redundancy, never trusting one copy. S3 never stores your object on a single disk. It spreads the data across many devices, and critically, across multiple separate facilities, data centers far enough apart, called availability zones, that a fire, flood, power loss, or network cut in one can't take out the others. So losing your data would require many independent failures across physically separated locations at the same time, which is wildly less likely than any single failure. The technique for spreading it efficiently is often erasure coding, the scheme we discussed: rather than keeping several full copies, the data is split into chunks with extra parity chunks computed, and spread across many devices and facilities, so that a number of them can be lost entirely and the original is still fully reconstructable from the survivors. This gives strong protection for far less storage overhead than whole copies, and it's how you survive multiple simultaneous failures by design.

The second pillar is that redundancy alone isn't enough, because data can rot silently. A bit on a disk can flip from age, from a manufacturing flaw, or, as we saw with the Belgian election, even from a cosmic ray, and if nobody checks, you might not notice until you go to read it and find it corrupt. So S3 continuously verifies integrity. When data is stored, a checksum, a fingerprint, is computed and kept. In the background, the system constantly reads stored data and re-checks it against its checksum, scanning for corruption or missing pieces, a process sometimes called scrubbing. The moment it detects that a disk has died or a chunk has gone bad, it automatically rebuilds the lost piece from the redundant chunks, using the erasure-coding math, and restores full redundancy. So the system is always healing, detecting failures and repairing them faster than new failures accumulate. Durability comes from this race: repair must stay ahead of failure, always.

Now you can see where the eleven nines actually comes from, and it's the key insight. It's a statistical result, not a guarantee any single piece of hardware provides. You know the rate at which disks fail. You know how many independent copies or chunks you keep, across how many separate facilities. You know how fast you can detect and rebuild a lost piece. From those, you can calculate the probability that enough failures strike the same object, in separate places, within the small window before repair completes, to actually lose it. By adding enough redundancy and spreading it across enough independent locations, and repairing fast, you drive that probability down to one in a hundred billion per year. Eleven nines isn't a disk being reliable, it's the computed odds of correlated failures outrunning your repairs, pushed to near zero by design.

And that's why the independence matters so much, the subtlety we noted with disks: these failures must be unlikely to happen together. If all your copies were in one building, one disaster takes them all, and your fancy math is worthless. So the copies are deliberately placed across separate facilities, separate power, separate everything, so that failures are as independent as possible, and the probability of enough of them coinciding becomes astronomically small. Engineering independence into the failures is as important as the redundancy itself.

Eleven nines, one lost file in ten thousand years, built from disks that die every day. Not because the disks are good, but because the data is spread across many of them in many places, constantly checked, and instantly rebuilt, so that losing it would take more bad luck, all at once, than the universe is likely to provide.
