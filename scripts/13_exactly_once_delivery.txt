Exactly-once delivery is mathematically impossible. Yet every payment system seems to offer it. Here's the trick behind the lie, and it starts with two armies.

In the Two Generals problem, two armies sit on opposite sides of a valley. They'll only win if they attack at the same time. They can only communicate by messenger, and any messenger might be captured.

General A sends: attack at dawn. But did it arrive? A can't know unless B confirms. So B sends back: got it. But did that arrive? B can't know unless A confirms the confirmation. And that message can be lost too. This goes on forever. No number of messages ever gives both sides certainty.

Your servers have the same problem. When a sender transmits a message and hears nothing back, it can't tell whether the message was lost or only the acknowledgement was lost.

So it has two choices. At-most-once: send it once and never retry. If it gets lost, it's gone forever. Or at-least-once: keep retrying until you hear back. Nothing is lost, but the receiver may get the same message several times.

There is no third option where the network guarantees exactly one delivery.

So how do payment systems promise it? They don't deliver once. They deliver many times, and make the duplicates harmless.

Every message carries a unique ID. The receiver keeps track of the IDs it has already handled. If a duplicate arrives, it recognises the ID and ignores it. The message may be delivered three times, but its effect is applied once.

That's the real name for it: effectively-once processing. At-least-once delivery, plus an idempotent receiver. Kafka's famous exactly-once feature works this way inside its own system, using sequence numbers and transactions.

You can't make the network deliver exactly once. You can make the receiver act exactly once.
