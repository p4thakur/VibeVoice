Page 1 loads in 2 milliseconds. Page 10,000 takes seconds. Same query, same table. This is the OFFSET trap, and almost every app falls into it.

Here's the query behind a typical page: select from posts, order by id, limit 10, offset 100,000. It reads naturally: skip 100,000 rows, give me the next 10.

But the database can't skip anything. It has no way to know where row 100,001 starts without counting. So it reads the first 100,000 rows, throws them away, and only then returns your 10.

That's the whole problem. Page 1 reads 10 rows. Page 100 reads 1,000. Page 10,000 reads 100,000 rows to show you 10. The cost grows with every page you go deeper, even though the answer is always 10 rows.

There's a second bug, and it's quieter. While you're reading page 3, someone adds a new post. Every row shifts by one. Page 4 now starts with a row you already saw on page 3. Or if a row is deleted, you skip one without knowing.

The fix is called keyset pagination, or cursor pagination. Instead of saying skip 100,000 rows, you say: give me the 10 rows after the last one I saw. In SQL, that's where id is less than the last id you received, order by id descending, limit 10.

Now the database uses its index to jump straight to that id, like opening a book to a bookmark. It reads exactly 10 rows. Page 1 and page 10,000 cost the same.

And since you're anchored to a row and not a position, new posts can't shift your place.

The trade-off: you can't jump straight to page 500. You can only go to next. That's fine for feeds and APIs. It's why Stripe, Slack and most modern APIs hand you a cursor instead of a page number.

Stop counting rows to skip. Remember where you were.
