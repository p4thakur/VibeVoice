A missile defense system missed an incoming missile in 1991 because it couldn't store the number 0.1 exactly. That small error cost 28 lives.

It's February 25, 1991, during the Gulf War, in Dhahran, Saudi Arabia. An Iraqi Scud missile is flying toward a US Army barracks. A Patriot battery is supposed to shoot it down. It never fires. The Scud hits, and 28 soldiers are killed.

The Patriot battery had been running continuously for about 100 hours. And that mattered.

Here's why. The system measured time in tenths of a second. It counted ticks as a whole number, then multiplied by 0.1 to turn them into seconds.

But in binary, 0.1 can't be written exactly. It's like 1 divided by 3 in decimal: 0.3333, going on forever. The computer has to cut it off somewhere. The Patriot stored it in a 24-bit register, so it chopped it off, and every tick carried a tiny error: about one ten-millionth of a second.

That's nothing. Unless you add it up. After 100 hours, there had been 3.6 million ticks, and the clock had drifted by about 0.34 seconds.

Now put that next to a Scud missile, which travels at roughly 1,700 meters per second. In 0.34 seconds, it moves over half a kilometer. The Patriot looked for the incoming missile in the place where it was supposed to be, saw nothing, and decided there was no threat.

The problem had been spotted before. A software fix was on its way, and reached the base the day after the attack.

An error that's too small to see in one second becomes deadly after 100 hours. And when you use numbers a computer can't store exactly, the error doesn't go away. It waits, and it grows.
